Teaching AI transformation by letting students run one

AI transformation is taught as slides and frameworks. Students can recite the sequence and still not feel why sequencing matters. This is a 60-minute class built so they find out by spending a budget.

01 The gap

Our course already uses games for accounting, supply chain, negotiation, entrepreneurship, operations, finance, leadership and strategy — drawn from SMU, MIT Sloan, Wharton, Darden and three free Android titles.

None of them teaches IT and AI transformation.

Students meet the topic as lecture slides and frameworks. They never take a sequencing decision, commit a budget to a foundation they cannot yet see a return on, or live with the debt of a shortcut. That is the gap this class fills.

02 The approach

Four pedagogies in sequence. Students play first, reflect on what happened, link it to the concept, then apply it to real cases.

Game

The main method. A board game where the only way to learn is to decide.

Role play

Simulation

Budgets, event cards and scores imitate the texture of a real transformation.

Debrief

Twelve minutes making explicit what the gameplay taught implicitly.

Debrief report

04 The mechanic that does the teaching

A use case earns value only when the capabilities it depends on are owned. This one rule carries the whole lesson.

Pilot

A prerequisite is missing. The use case pays nothing until the last one is bought.

Live

All prerequisites owned. Pays its full value every round.

Fast-tracked

Launched early with gaps. Pays now, but adds one debt token per gap.

And until a team owns the Change Management Office, every use case pays only half its value. Tools without change management are worth less than their pilot claimed.

Event cards are symmetric

One card is revealed to the whole class each round from round 2. The same card rewards a prepared team and punishes an unprepared one—which is how governance and security stop feeling like overhead.

The winner is not the richest team

Transformation Score combines three things. During play the leaderboard shows value only; the weights stay hidden until the final reveal, so the team that looked richest is often overtaken.

Value earned+Customer trust−Technical debt

Trust starts at 5 and moves with events and the regulator's audit. One debt token is charged for every shortcut still unpaid at the end. Do not reveal the weights before the game ends.

Worked illustration, not class data

Two teams run the same retail chain under the starting rules, one buying foundations first and one fast-tracking from round one. They are level until round four; the foundation-first team then pulls away. Final value: 58 against 39. The rushing team spends 18 budget points on debt interest and its largest use case never goes live.

This is a hand-worked example that leaves out dice, fees and events. Real games vary.

06 The case video

Before the game, students meet the argument as a parable: Baron Coburg — the first AI ROI case? A landlord, two farmers, and an infographic that does not survive scrutiny.

The story in three lines

Ivan brings in 223 bushels — the bigger harvest — but his plough is broken beyond repair and he still owes the ploughmaker. Frederick brings in only 105, but his equipment is preserved for more seasons. The infographic reports 10.7 against 12.2 net per acre and calls Frederick about 14% better.

Then it turns on the arithmetic

The video's real move is that those figures do not reconcile with the case data. It says so on screen: unresolved, not verified, illustrative rather than a calculation of return. The point is not that the numbers are wrong—it is that a flashy pilot is not evidence of a return.

The translation students should leave with

The feudal storyIn an AI programme
Bushels harvestedDemo vanity metrics
Seed and fertiliserTokens and compute
The oxShared platform infrastructure
The ploughScaffolding and tech debt
The ploughmaker's billUnpaid vendor liabilities

Don't judge an AI pilot by its flashy demo. Count the costs, the debt, and what's left standing afterwards.

07 Why the video and the game sit together

They teach the same idea from opposite ends. The video states the argument; the game makes students pay for getting it wrong.

The video gives the parable

  • Output is not return
  • Hidden debt outlives the project
  • Vendor liabilities are real liabilities
  • The infographic can be wrong and still persuasive

The game makes it cost something

  • Fast-track a use case, take a debt token every round
  • Skip Change Management, every use case pays half
  • Skip governance, the next event card bills you
  • The final score counts debt nobody saw during play

One facilitation note: the video spends time on the arithmetic discrepancy. Make sure students leave with “count the debt” rather than “the numbers were wrong”.

08 The 60-minute run

0–3
Join

Form teams, enter the code, pick roles

3–8
Briefing

Rules and one worked example

8–46
Play

Eight rounds of 4 min 45 s — roll 15 s, decide 2 min 30 s, event 60 s, payout 60 s

46–48
Reveal

Final scores, one component at a time

48–60
Debrief

Discuss what won and why

All teams play every phase at the same time, so nobody waits for a turn. Round one has no event card. If the class runs late, cut to six rounds—but do not shorten the debrief, because that is where the learning is made explicit.

Three questions to open the debrief

  1. Which team led after round 3, and which team won?
  2. Who fast-tracked a use case? Was it worth the debt?
  3. What would you buy first if you played again?
